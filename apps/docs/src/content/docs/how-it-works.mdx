Counting rods were short sticks of bamboo or bone laid out on a counting board. Chinese mathematicians calculated with them for around two thousand years, and wrote numbers down by drawing the rods.

## The digits

One to five are that many parallel rods. Six to nine put one crossing rod, worth five, above one to four parallel rods.

Units, hundreds and every even place use **upright rods**:

Tens, thousands and every odd place turn them over and use **flat rods**:

## Places

Places run left to right, most significant first. Alternating the orientation keeps neighbouring digits apart: 11 is a flat rod and then an upright one, so it can't be read as 2.

## Zero

The earliest rod numerals left an empty place for zero. From the 13th century, mathematicians such as Qin Jiushao drew a circle instead. This library draws the circle by default; `zero: 'blank'` leaves the gap.

  <Glyph value={305} caption="305" />

</div>

## Negatives

Counting boards used red rods for positive numbers and black rods for negative ones. In writing, the Song-dynasty mathematician Li Ye struck a diagonal rod across the last digit instead. This library draws the diagonal rod, and `traditionalColors` adds the red and black.

## Decimals

Rod numerals marked fractions with a unit word under the units digit. This library uses a dot on the baseline instead, and the fraction places keep alternating.
